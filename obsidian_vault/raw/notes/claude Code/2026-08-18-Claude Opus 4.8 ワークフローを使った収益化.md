# 80 Claude Opus 4.8 Workflows That Make Money While You Sleep

## Most people open Claude, ask one question, read the answer, close the tab.

That is not what this is about.

This is about the other use case. Claude as a system that runs without you, makes decisions inside boundaries you set once, and produces work or income while you sleep. Opus 4.8 fits this specifically well: efficient enough for high volume, reliable enough to trust with real, repeated work.

Here is why the model choice matters for a list like this specifically. A workflow that runs once a week barely notices which model powers it. A workflow that runs dozens of times a day, every single day, for months, is a completely different cost equation. Opus 4.8 sits at a price and capability point that makes running genuinely high volume, repeated automation actually sustainable, without needing to reach for a premium reasoning model on tasks that never required that depth in the first place.

Here are 80 of them, across 16 categories. Every one is a trigger, an agent, and a verification step. Skip the verification and you get expensive mistakes running unattended. Keep it and you get a system you can trust overnight. None of these are Claude being smarter than you. All of them are Claude removing you from work that never needed you present in real time.

## The Pattern Behind All Eighty

Every one of these runs on the same three parts. A trigger, a schedule, a new file, an incoming message. A capable agent with the right tools and context. A verification step checking the output before it reaches you or goes live.

Miss the verification and you get expensive mistakes running unattended. Miss the trigger and you are starting everything by hand again. Build all three and you have a system that genuinely works overnight, not a demo that only works while you watch it.

## Why These Categories Specifically, And What Connects Them

Looking across all sixteen categories, a pattern shows up worth naming directly, since it's the actual insight behind why this list works as a whole rather than as eighty disconnected ideas.

Every single category is built around work that has three properties simultaneously. It's repeated, meaning you do some version of it regularly, not as a one-off. It has a checkable definition of done, meaning you or a verification step can actually tell whether it was completed correctly, not just plausibly. And it doesn't require judgment that only you have access to, meaning the actual decision-making, not just the execution, can be handed off within defined boundaries.

Tasks that fail any one of these three tests don't belong on a list like this, and it's worth understanding why, since it tells you how to evaluate whether a task in your own work is a good candidate for automation beyond the eighty specific examples above. A one-off task, no matter how tedious, doesn't justify building a system around it, the setup cost isn't recovered. A task with no checkable definition of done can't be safely automated regardless of how repetitive it is, because you have no way to verify it's actually working correctly without watching every single instance. And a task requiring judgment genuinely unique to you, reading a specific person's tone in a sensitive negotiation, making a call that depends on relationship history no system has access to, shouldn't be automated even if it's repeated and checkable, because the verification standard itself would have to be you, defeating the purpose.

## Routing Workflows To The Right Model, Not Just Opus 4.8 By Default

This list is framed around Opus 4.8 specifically, and for the large majority of the eighty workflows above, that's the right default. But it's worth being honest about where that default should actually shift, since blindly running everything through one model regardless of task difficulty is its own kind of waste.

For the categories involving routine extraction and formatting, invoice drafting, expense categorization, product description generation, changelog drafting, Opus 4.8 is not just adequate, it's likely more cost-efficient than reaching for a more expensive reasoning model that offers no real benefit on tasks this well-defined.

For the categories involving genuinely difficult judgment calls under ambiguity, due diligence first passes on complex documents, contract redlines with real legal nuance, deep research synthesis across contradictory sources, it's worth testing whether a more capable model produces a meaningfully better result on your specific real cases before assuming Opus 4.8's default performance is sufficient. The cost difference on an occasional, high-stakes task is rarely the deciding factor, the quality difference is.

For the categories involving pure volume with minimal per-task complexity, competitor monitoring, pricing checks, sentiment scanning, an even lighter, cheaper model than Opus 4.8 may perform just as reliably, and running dozens of these checks daily at a lower per-call cost compounds meaningfully over a month in a way a single expensive call never would.

The actual discipline worth adopting: don't pick one model for your entire automation stack. Match the model to what each specific category of task genuinely requires, and revisit that match periodically as pricing and capability shift, rather than locking in a decision made once and never revisited.

## Troubleshooting The Failure Modes That Actually Show Up

A handful of specific problems account for most of the friction people report when building out several of these workflows at once, worth knowing the fix for in advance.

A workflow that worked in testing starts producing worse output after a few weeks of real use. This is almost always a signal that the input data has drifted from what the workflow was originally built and tested against, a new competitor entered your monitoring list with a different site structure, your product catalog added a category the description generator wasn't tuned for. Periodically re-testing a workflow against fresh, real examples, not just trusting that a system tested once stays correct forever, catches this before it becomes a real problem.

Verification steps start feeling like they're just rubber-stamping everything. If a Judge or checking step has approved one hundred outputs in a row without ever flagging an issue, that's worth investigating directly rather than treating as a sign of a flawless system. Deliberately feed it a known-bad example periodically and confirm it still actually catches the problem, the same stress-testing discipline recommended for any verification system generally.

Running many of these workflows simultaneously makes it hard to know which one is actually costing the most. Track cost per workflow separately, not just an aggregate monthly bill. A workflow that seemed cheap individually can become the largest cost driver once it's running dozens of times a day, and you won't notice unless you're actually tracking per-workflow cost, not just a total number that obscures which specific system is driving it.

## A Worked Example: Combining Several Workflows Into One System

The eighty items above are listed individually, but the real leverage shows up when several of them chain together into one coherent system rather than running as isolated, disconnected automations. Here's a realistic example of how that looks for a small e-commerce operation.

Workflow 6, the prospecting list builder, runs weekly, surfacing new potential wholesale accounts matching specific criteria. Workflow 16, the cold outreach personalizer, picks up that list and drafts a genuinely tailored opener for each one, referencing something specific about their actual business rather than a generic template. Workflow 17, the inbound lead qualifier, handles the replies that come back, tagging each one hot, warm, or cold before a human looks at any of it.

Separately, workflow 46, the product description generator, keeps the catalog current as new products get added, while workflow 48, the inventory reorder alert, and workflow 49, the pricing monitor, run in the background checking stock levels and competitor pricing daily without anyone remembering to check manually.

None of these five workflows individually looks like a dramatic transformation. Together, they represent the entire top-of-funnel sales motion and a meaningful share of ongoing catalog maintenance running with the business owner touching only the qualified, warm leads and the occasional pricing decision that genuinely needs human judgment. That's the actual shape of what "eighty workflows" is pointing toward, not eighty separate, disconnected automations, but a smaller number of connected systems built from combining the individual building blocks above into something that matches your actual business.

The practical lesson from this example: don't evaluate any single workflow in isolation from what it could connect to. When you're deciding which of the eighty to build first, also ask which two or three naturally chain together into a system bigger than the sum of its parts, since that combination is usually where the real time savings compound.

## Deciding Which Of The Eighty To Build First

With eighty options, the actual bottleneck for most people isn't lacking ideas, it's picking one and actually finishing it rather than starting several and finishing none. Here's a concrete way to decide.

Rank your candidates by a simple two-factor comparison: how often you currently do this task manually, and how painful or error-prone doing it manually actually is. A task you do daily that's also genuinely tedious, email triage, competitor monitoring, lead qualification, sits at the top of that ranking regardless of which category it falls under. A task you do monthly and don't particularly mind doing by hand sits at the bottom, even if automating it sounds impressive.

Within your top few candidates, apply one more filter: which one has the clearest, most checkable definition of done. A workflow where you can write down exactly what correct output looks like, in a sentence or two, is faster to build and safer to trust than one where "good output" is a vaguer, more subjective judgment call. Start with the clearest one, not necessarily the most exciting one.

Resist the urge to pick a workflow because it sounds impressive to talk about rather than because it actually addresses your real daily friction. The workflows that end up mattering most in practice are rarely the most sophisticated-sounding ones on this list. They're the boring, repeated, checkable tasks that quietly ate an hour of every single day without anyone noticing how much that hour actually cost over a month.

## What This List Doesn't Replace

Worth being direct about the limits here, since a list this long can start to feel like it implies full automation of an entire business, and that overstates what's actually true.

Every workflow above handles execution of well-defined, repeated work. None of them replace the strategic decisions about what your business should actually be doing, which market to pursue, which product to build next, which relationship is worth investing real personal time in. Those decisions still require you, specifically, not because a model couldn't generate a plausible-sounding answer, but because the actual accountability and judgment behind a strategic call isn't something that should live inside an unattended system regardless of how good the underlying model is.

The honest way to think about the relationship between these eighty workflows and the strategic side of running something: automation clears the operational noise that competes for your attention, so the time and mental space you get back is available for the decisions that actually need a human making them. It's not a replacement for that judgment. It's what makes room for it.

## What This Actually Buys You Over Time

The value of building even a handful of these compounds in a way that's easy to underestimate looking at any single workflow in isolation. A single automated task saving twenty minutes a day sounds modest. Ten of them running simultaneously, each saving a similar amount, adds up to hours reclaimed every single week, hours that used to disappear into repeated, checkable work that never actually needed your personal attention in the first place.

The real compounding effect shows up specifically in month two or three, once several workflows are running reliably at the same time and you notice the shape of your actual workday has changed, less time spent on the mechanical, repeated parts of running things, more time available for the parts that genuinely benefit from your specific judgment and attention. That shift, not any single workflow's individual output, is the actual thing this entire list is pointing toward.

## How To Actually Start

Pick one workflow. The one that maps onto something you already do every single day. Get it running reliably for a week before adding a second. Reliable means it ran without you checking constantly and the output was good enough to use without heavy editing.

The people who build five fragile systems babysit all five. The people who build one solid system build the next nine faster, because they already know exactly what breaks and why.

That compounding is the real unlock. Not the first workflow. The tenth, built in a fraction of the time the first one took.

Pick one. Build it properly. The other seventy nine get easier once the first one actually runs, and the connections between them, the way workflows chain into real systems rather than staying isolated, are where the actual time savings compound past what any individual item on this list can deliver alone.
